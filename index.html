  • Explicit goal and intervention
  • Defined group size/dosage

Role-specific MTSS pathways

Everyone participates in MTSS, but not everyone owns the same decisions. These pathways clarify the work expected from common GRPS roles.

Principal / Building Leader

Owns the system.

Protect Tier 1 quality, establish meeting cadence, ensure intervention capacity, remove implementation barriers, monitor fidelity and outcomes, and prevent MTSS from becoming a referral-only process.

Classroom Teacher

Owns daily Tier 1 access and evidence.

Differentiate core instruction/supports, notice patterns, document classroom strategies and response, contribute precise evidence, implement assigned supports, and communicate with families.

MTSS Scholar Success Teacher

Owns targeted intervention delivery and monitoring.

Deliver supplemental academic/behavioral/SEL supports, maintain intervention documentation, progress monitor, coordinate with classroom staff and families, and surface fidelity or capacity concerns.

FSS / KSSN / Support Staff

Owns barrier removal and coordinated supports.

Identify nonacademic barriers, connect scholars/families to school and community resources, coordinate attendance and wellness plans, bring family voice to problem solving, and track whether the barrier is actually being reduced.

Entry, intensification, fading and exit

Tier labels should describe the intensity of support—not a destination or permanent placement.

DecisionEvidence to requireWhat the team does
Enter Tier 2Specific need/barrier is defined; Tier 1 access has been verified; data show the scholar needs supplemental support.Select a matched intervention with explicit dosage, owner, goal, progress measure and review date.
ContinueProgress trend is adequate and fidelity is strong.Continue through the planned duration; do not change a working intervention prematurely.
Adjust / IntensifyProgress is inadequate while fidelity is strong.Increase precision or intensity: smaller group, more time, increased frequency, stronger match, added component, or Tier 3 problem solving.
Repair FidelityProgress is inadequate and implementation is inconsistent.Fix scheduling, staffing, training, dosage or critical components before declaring the intervention ineffective.

Important: Tier 3 does not automatically mean special education, and special education is not a “Tier 4.” Evaluation decisions follow applicable legal criteria and comprehensive evidence, not a simple sequence of failed tiers.

Tier 1 schoolwide analysis

Before individualizing, teams should test whether the pattern is actually a system problem.

1. Who is affected?

Disaggregate by grade, classroom/course, subgroup, time, location and setting. Look for concentration rather than relying on one aggregate rate.

2. Is Tier 1 accessible and implemented?

Check instructional/support expectations, schedule, opportunity to learn, climate, attendance conditions, staffing and fidelity.

3. What is the smallest system change likely to help?

Select a response that changes the environment, instruction, routine or adult practice—then measure implementation and outcomes.

What an implementation-ready plan looks like

EXAMPLE · ATTENDANCE

Scholar misses first period 2–3 days each week

Need/barrier: Morning transportation is unreliable after a family schedule change. The primary issue is access—not motivation.

Evidence: 9 first-period absences in 20 school days; later periods are attended at 94%; scholar/family interview confirms transportation barrier.

Intervention: Family + FSS develop a reliable primary and backup transportation plan; designated adult checks arrival for 10 school days and troubleshoots missed connections the same day.

Owner / dosage: FSS coordinates plan; daily arrival check for 10 school days.

Progress measure: First-period attendance daily. Fidelity: transportation plan available and check completed each school day.

Decision rule: If first-period attendance reaches ≥90% for 10 consecutive school days, fade daily check to twice weekly. If attendance remains below 80% with full fidelity, reconvene to identify an additional or different barrier.

One MTSS Workflow

Regardless of domain, use the same problem-solving sequence. Start with the concern, determine whether the problem is primarily Tier 1/system-level or scholar/small-group level, match support to the defined need, implement and monitor, then make the next decision from evidence.

1. DefineWhat specifically is happening, for whom, where, and compared with what expectation? 2. UnderstandUse the smallest useful set of evidence to identify the likely skill, barrier, function, or system condition. 3. MatchChoose a support that directly addresses the defined need—not simply a familiar program or referral destination. 4. Implement + MonitorSet dosage, ownership, progress measure, fidelity/access check, review date, and decision rule. 5. DecideContinue, modify, intensify, fade, exit, or address a system barrier based on response and implementation evidence.
Core guardrail: Tier 2 and Tier 3 are not places where scholars are sent. They describe increasing levels of support. When many scholars share the same problem, investigate Tier 1 and system conditions before creating individual plans.
GRPS model

How MTSS Works in GRPS

GRPS organizes MTSS across four connected areas. Teams use the same problem-solving process across domains, while supports may differ in intensity depending on the scholar or system need.

Academics

High-quality core instruction, screening, differentiation, targeted intervention, progress monitoring, and intensification.

Attendance

Universal prevention, early identification, root-cause inquiry, barrier removal, engagement, and individualized attendance support.

Mental Health / SEL

Belonging, explicit SEL instruction, targeted supports, referral/coordination, and appropriate intensive or community-based services.

PBIS / Behavior

Schoolwide expectations, restorative and preventive practices, targeted behavior supports, and individualized problem solving.

Tier 1 — UniversalStrong core instruction and schoolwide supports for all scholars, with screening and differentiation.
Tier 2 — TargetedAdditional strategic support matched to a defined need while Tier 1 continues.
Tier 3 — IntensiveMore frequent, individualized, and coordinated support based on need and response data.
Key GRPS principle: Equity, inclusion, SEL, and data-based decision making run through the entire framework. Scholars may need different levels of support in different domains at the same time.

  • Allocates resources and develops professional learning.
  • Builds implementation capacity across schools.
  • Removes district-level barriers that prevent full implementation.
  • Monitors patterns across buildings and addresses system conditions.

GRPS operating model

Who Solves What?

Use the lowest level of the system that can effectively solve the problem. Move a concern upward when additional coordination, authority, resources, expertise, or system change is required—not simply because a scholar is struggling.

Level 1

Classroom / Grade / Content Team

  • Strengthens Tier 1 instruction, routines, relationships, and differentiation.
  • Notices patterns and defines concerns.
  • Uses readily available data and scholar/family information.
  • Implements reasonable classroom/team supports.
  • Identifies when coordinated problem solving is needed.
Level 2

Student Success Team

  • Solves defined scholar-level or small-group problems requiring coordination.
  • Clarifies root cause/need and matches intervention.
  • Coordinates academic and nonacademic supports.
  • Sets owner, dosage, monitoring, fidelity, review date, and decision rule.
  • Intensifies deliberately when evidence supports it.
Level 3

Building MTSS Leadership Team

  • Owns buildingwide MTSS implementation.
  • Reviews Tier 1 health and schoolwide/subgroup patterns.
  • Manages intervention capacity, schedules, staffing, and access.
  • Monitors whether SST and intervention systems are functioning.
  • Removes building-level implementation barriers.
Level 4

District MTSS Support

  • Establishes common framework, standards, tools, and expectations.
  • Builds school capacity through professional learning and coaching.
  • Reviews cross-school patterns and implementation data.
  • Allocates/aligns resources and removes district-level barriers.
  • Addresses issues requiring district authority or cross-department coordination.
Important: This is not a referral ladder. A concern does not have to travel through every level. Building and district teams may act directly when data reveal a system-level problem.

GRPS problem-solving process

From Concern to Decision

The purpose of MTSS is not to move scholars through tiers. It is to solve a defined problem with the least intensive support likely to work, implement it well, and use evidence to decide what happens next.

Student Success Team

When the Student Success Team Adds Value

Use the Student Success Team when the concern requires coordinated problem solving beyond routine classroom or grade-level support, when response is unclear, or when multiple domains or partners must be aligned.

Bring these six things

1. Clear concern
Observable and measurable, not a label.
2. Current evidence
Outcome data plus relevant qualitative evidence.
3. Tier 1 context
What core supports and differentiation are already in place?
4. What has been tried
Include implementation details, not just a list of actions.
5. Scholar/family voice
What does the scholar or family say is helping or getting in the way?
6. Decision needed
What must the team decide today?

Every SST meeting should end with

  • A precise problem/root-cause statement.
  • A matched intervention or system action.
  • A named owner and partner roles.
  • Frequency/dosage, duration, and start date.
  • A progress-monitoring measure and fidelity check.
  • A review date and explicit decision rule.
If the team cannot name these items, the plan is not yet implementation-ready.
Data cadence

Review the Right Data at the Right Level

MTSS data review should distinguish schoolwide patterns from individual response. This prevents buildings from using scholar-level meetings to solve Tier 1 or organizational problems.

Universal / Benchmark Review

Typical cadence: Fall, winter, spring and after major benchmark windows.

Ask: Are enough scholars succeeding with Tier 1? Where are subgroup, grade, course, attendance, belonging, or behavior patterns concentrated?

Decision: Strengthen core/system conditions before creating unnecessary individual plans.

Targeted Support Review

Typical cadence: Regular grade/content/team review based on intervention cycle.

Ask: Is the support reaching the right scholars, delivered at the intended dosage, and producing the expected rate of progress?

Decision: Continue, correct fidelity, modify, intensify, or fade.

Intensive / Individual Review

Typical cadence: More frequent, based on need and intervention design.

Ask: What does the combined outcome + fidelity + access + experience evidence show?

Decision: Individualize or intensify deliberately; coordinate across domains and services as needed.

Assessment system

Use Assessment to Answer a Question

Assessment is useful only when the team is clear about the decision it needs to make. GRPS teams should use the smallest amount of evidence necessary to answer the question reliably.

Universal screening

Purpose: Identify schoolwide strengths, needs, and scholars who may require additional support.

Use for: Tier 1 evaluation, early identification, subgroup analysis, and intervention capacity planning.

Do not use for: Making a complete diagnosis from one score.

Diagnostic / root-cause evidence

Purpose: Understand the specific skill gap, barrier, function, or condition contributing to the concern.

Use for: Selecting or adapting an intervention.

Do not use for: Delaying support while teams collect unnecessary data.

Progress monitoring

Purpose: Determine whether the scholar is responding to the intervention at the expected rate.

Use for: Continue, change, intensify, or fade decisions.

Do not use for: Monitoring unrelated outcomes that do not reflect the target skill or barrier.

Fidelity / implementation evidence

Purpose: Verify that the support was actually delivered as designed.

Use for: Interpreting outcome data and identifying implementation barriers.

Do not use for: Punitive evaluation of individual staff.

Decision rule: Never conclude that an intervention “did not work” until the team can reasonably verify adequate access, sufficient time, and acceptable implementation fidelity.
Intervention planning

The GRPS Intervention Plan Standard

A strong plan is specific enough that another trained staff member could understand what is supposed to happen, who is responsible, and how the team will know whether it worked.

Define the need. State the academic skill, attendance barrier, behavioral function/pattern, or SEL/mental-health-related need precisely.
State the goal. Define the measurable change expected by the review date.
Name the intervention. Identify the support and explain why it matches the need.
Define dosage. Frequency, minutes, group size, setting, and duration.
Name the owner. One lead person is accountable for ensuring implementation occurs; partners may share tasks.
Define the progress measure. Specify what evidence will show scholar response.
Define the fidelity measure. Identify how the team will verify implementation.
Set the review date. Choose a date aligned to the intervention cycle—not an arbitrary calendar date.
Set the decision rule. State in advance what evidence will trigger continue, change, intensify, fade, or exit.
Family partnership

Families Are Partners in Problem Solving

Family engagement should improve the team's understanding of the scholar's strengths, experience, barriers, and response to support. It should not be reduced to notifying families after decisions have already been made.

Before the plan

  • Share the concern in clear, nontechnical language.
  • Ask what the family and scholar are noticing.
  • Identify strengths, successful routines, cultural/linguistic considerations, and barriers.
  • Clarify what the school is already doing at Tier 1.

During implementation

  • Explain the intervention, goal, owner, and review date.
  • Agree on the most useful communication method and frequency.
  • Avoid repeated requests for the family to retell the same story to multiple staff.
  • Ask whether the support is improving the scholar's actual experience.

At review

  • Share progress and fidelity evidence.
  • Ask whether the original barrier or concern has changed.
  • Discuss continue / modify / intensify / fade decisions before finalizing them.
  • Document new responsibilities and next review point.

Communication standard

Communication should be understandable, respectful, culturally responsive, and available in the family's preferred language when needed.

A family contact is not automatically an intervention. It becomes part of an intervention only when it contributes directly to changing the identified barrier or condition.

Documentation

Document the Decision, Not Just the Activity

Useful MTSS documentation should allow another team member to understand what problem was identified, what support was selected, whether it happened, what changed, and what decision was made.

Minimum case record

  • Concern/problem statement
  • Root-cause or need hypothesis
  • Intervention and rationale
  • Owner + partners
  • Start date + dosage + duration
  • Progress measure + fidelity measure
  • Review date
  • Decision + next step

Avoid documentation overload

  • Do not duplicate the same plan across multiple tools unless required.
  • Do not document every contact as if it were a separate intervention.
  • Do not collect information the team will not use to make a decision.
  • Keep protected information limited to what is necessary for the educational purpose.

Example: Attendance Barrier-Removal Plan

Concern
Scholar attended 74% of the last 20 instructional days.
Evidence
Four of five recent absences occurred when the family's usual transportation was unavailable; family interview confirms transportation instability.
Intervention
Establish reliable morning transportation plus a backup plan for 20 instructional days.
Owner
FSS coordinates the plan; family confirms changes; school attendance contact reviews successful access weekly.
Monitor
Days present, planned rides completed, and family report that the transportation barrier is decreasing.
Decision
Continue/fade when access is stable and attendance improves; modify if rides are not occurring; investigate other causes if access is stable but attendance remains low.

Example: Tier 2 Reading Plan

Need
Diagnostic evidence indicates a decoding deficit rather than a general “low reading” concern.
Goal
Meet the established decoding progress target by the six-week review.
Intervention
Evidence-based explicit decoding intervention aligned to the identified skill need.
Dosage
4 × 30 minutes/week, group of four, for six weeks.
Monitor
Weekly skill-aligned progress monitoring plus intervention attendance and fidelity.
Decision
Continue if response is adequate; repair fidelity/access first when weak; intensify deliberately when adequate fidelity/access and data show inadequate response.

Example: Strong SST Decision

Problem
Scholar completes fewer than 30% of independent mathematics tasks requiring multi-step computation.
Hypothesis
Work samples and brief diagnostic evidence indicate prerequisite computation gaps; disruption occurs primarily after difficult tasks are assigned.
Decision
Provide targeted prerequisite-skill instruction while adjusting independent-task entry points and explicitly teaching a help-seeking routine.
Ownership
Interventionist provides skill support; teacher implements task/access adjustments and help-seeking routine.
Evidence
Skill probe, task completion, replacement behavior, intervention exposure, fidelity.
Review
Team reviews at the end of the defined intervention cycle using the pre-established decision rule.

Example: Deliberate Intensification

Before changing
Team confirms sufficient intervention time, acceptable fidelity, adequate attendance/access, and enough progress data to identify a trend.
Finding
Scholar is not on track to meet the goal despite adequate implementation.
Adaptation
Increase opportunities to respond and feedback, reduce group size, and strengthen explicitness while keeping the validated intervention platform aligned to the same need.
Monitoring
Add a phase line/start date; continue aligned progress monitoring and verify fidelity to the intensified plan.
Decision
Review response to the adaptation rather than changing multiple variables at once.

Example: Tier 1 System Response

Pattern
A large share of Grade 6 scholars are failing the same mathematics standard.
Wrong move
Create dozens of Tier 2 referrals.
System inquiry
Review common assessment items, instructional opportunity, pacing, prerequisite skill demands, classroom implementation, and subgroup patterns.
Response
Strengthen/reteach the core instructional sequence and monitor classwide response.
Then
Identify scholars who remain below expectation after the Tier 1 response and determine whether supplemental support is warranted.
Grade-band application

MTSS Looks Different Across Grade Spans

PK–5

Emphasize foundational literacy/numeracy, classroom behavior/SEL routines, attendance habits, family partnership, early screening, and rapid skill-specific support. Progress monitoring should align tightly to the taught skill.

6–8

Add course performance, transition to multiple teachers/settings, belonging, engagement, attendance patterns, behavior across settings, and early-warning indicators. Coordinate supports so scholars are not overloaded with disconnected interventions.

9–12

Use attendance, course failures, credits/on-track status, behavior, engagement/belonging, graduation trajectory, and EWIMS-style early warning. Intervention plans should account for schedule, credit recovery/acceleration, postsecondary relevance, and scholar voice.

Secondary guardrail: A student can be “on track” in one indicator and at significant risk in another. Teams should examine patterns across attendance, behavior/engagement, and course/credit performance rather than relying on a single threshold.

GRPS academic MTSS

Start With Grade-Level Core. Add Intensity With Purpose.

The GRPS academic guidance describes Tier 1 as the grade-level core provided to all scholars, with Tier 2 supplemental to and aligned with Tier 1. Tier 3 provides greater intensity and remains aligned to Tier 1 and Tier 2. The source notes that intensity may change through conditions such as time, focus, frequency/duration, and group size.

Do not confuse a resource with an intervention. A curriculum feature, digital program, scaffold, differentiation strategy, or technology tool becomes an MTSS intervention only when the team defines the target need, implementation conditions/dosage, owner, progress measure, fidelity/access evidence, review date, and decision rule.

Tier 1 Academic Health: GRPS Essential Ingredients

Before creating large numbers of supplemental plans, examine whether rigorous grade-level instruction is consistently present. The academic guidance identifies seven GRPS essential ingredients:

Culturally affirming instruction
Standards-aligned learning targets unpacked, shared, and assessed with scholars
Scholar artifacts that illustrate modeling of thinking and ongoing learning
Classroom routines, norms, and expectations
Gradual release of responsibility: I do, we do, you do
Timely checking for understanding
Scholar talk/discourse, norms, and expectations
Tier 1 decision rule: When a pattern affects many scholars, first test whether core instruction, opportunity/access, implementation, or learning conditions need strengthening. Do not convert a Tier 1 problem into dozens of individual Tier 2 referrals.

Role clarity

What Does This Look Like in My Role?

Principal / Building Leader

1Protect Tier 1 implementation and ensure teams have usable outcome + fidelity data.
2Establish meeting cadence, roles, decision rules, intervention capacity, and escalation routes.
3Remove system barriers when multiple scholars show the same pattern.
4Monitor whether decisions are implemented—not just whether meetings occur.

Teacher

1Deliver and differentiate Tier 1; collect concise evidence of the defined concern.
2Implement agreed supports as designed and communicate implementation barriers early.
3Bring classroom evidence and scholar/family perspective to problem solving.
4Use the team's decision rule rather than waiting for the concern to become severe.

MTSS Scholar Success Teacher

1Help define the need precisely and match support to academic/non-academic evidence.
2Coordinate intervention implementation, progress monitoring, and fidelity evidence.
3Surface barriers and support data-based intensification or fading.
4Maintain usable documentation and connect families/resources when barriers extend beyond instruction.

FSS / KSSN / Support Staff

1Identify root barriers through scholar/family engagement rather than assuming cause.
2Convert information into a barrier-removal intervention with a named owner and follow-through.
3Coordinate school/community resources and reduce repeated family storytelling.
4Report whether the barrier changed and whether the scholar outcome changed.

Counselor / Social Worker / Behavioral Health

1Clarify social-emotional, behavioral, belonging, or mental-health need and urgency.
2Match supports to the identified need and define progress indicators.
3Coordinate with classroom and family systems so support is not isolated.
4Escalate safety/clinical concerns through appropriate procedures outside routine MTSS decision making.

District / Department Team

1Define the district's common MTSS architecture, measures, tools, and minimum expectations.
2Monitor school implementation and outcome patterns, not only compliance artifacts.
3Build intervention capacity and solve cross-school/system barriers.
4Use fidelity evidence to target professional learning, coaching, and resource allocation.

Before Adding a Resource to the Field Manual

1. Purpose

What specific MTSS task does this resource help a user complete?

2. Audience

Who should use it: leader, teacher, interventionist, support staff, scholar, or family?

3. Domain + Tier

Tag by Academics, Attendance, Behavior/PBIS, SEL/Mental Health and applicable tier(s).

4. Evidence / Authority

Prefer GRPS-approved, MDE/MiMTSS, or otherwise vetted evidence-based resources.

5. Usability

Users should know when to use it, how to use it, and what output it should produce.

Assign an owner and review date so the Field Manual does not become a graveyard of outdated links.
